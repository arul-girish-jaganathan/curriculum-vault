# IT Risk

## Purpose
Build an executive-grade understanding of **IT Risk** as part of a repeatable Operations & Execution system.

## Core Model
- **Objective:** define the measurable business or operational outcome.
- **Flow:** understand how work, information, decisions, money, materials, or customer demand move through the system.
- **Capacity:** identify the constrained people, capital, equipment, systems, skills, or leadership attention.
- **Controls:** define ownership, metrics, policies, thresholds, approvals, and escalation.
- **Feedback:** use performance, customer signals, variance, and failure data to improve the operating model.

## Key Questions
1. What outcome is this mechanism accountable for?
2. What are the inputs, outputs, handoffs, and constraints?
3. Where is the bottleneck or highest-risk dependency?
4. Which leading indicator gives enough warning to act?
5. Who owns the decision and who owns execution?
6. What happens when demand exceeds capacity or the normal process fails?

## Metrics
Use balanced measures such as throughput, cycle time, lead time, WIP, utilization, headroom, quality, cost, service level, customer outcomes, reliability, and employee/talent signals. Avoid local optimization that harms the system outcome.

## Execution Workflow
1. Define the objective and constraints.
2. Assign owners and decision rights.
3. Map the operating flow.
4. Identify bottlenecks and dependencies.
5. Establish a baseline.
6. Set targets and review cadence.
7. Create escalation and recovery paths.
8. Execute and measure.
9. Analyze variance and root causes.
10. Standardize improvements and close actions.

## Failure Modes
Consider unclear ownership, hidden queues, excessive WIP, local optimization, resource contention, poor forecasting, weak handoffs, decision latency, process drift, uncontrolled exceptions, metric gaming, recurring incidents, and external dependency failures.

## Risk and Resilience
Define preventive controls, early-warning signals, containment, fallback capacity, escalation paths, and recovery procedures. Critical operations should not depend on a single person, site, supplier, system, or undocumented process without an explicit risk decision.

## Cross-Functional Execution
Make interfaces explicit:
- input contract;
- expected output;
- timing;
- owner;
- quality criteria;
- dependencies;
- escalation.

Execution frequently fails at organizational interfaces rather than within isolated functions.

## Automation
Automate repeatable, deterministic work where it improves speed, consistency, or control. Preserve human judgment at decision points that require context or accountability. Measure automation reliability and failure recovery.

## Governance and Review
A good operating review focuses on what changed, why it changed, what is off target, what decision is needed, who owns the action, by when, and what evidence confirms closure.

## Scaling
As volume grows, decide what to standardize, centralize, automate, localize, delegate, or eliminate. Growth should not multiply coordination cost faster than value creation.

## Common Mistakes
- Measuring activity instead of outcomes.
- Adding resources before identifying the real constraint.
- Creating approval layers that slow decisions.
- Allowing exceptions to become the standard process.
- Reviewing metrics without assigning actions.
- Optimizing departmental targets at company-level expense.
- Confusing motion with execution.

## Staff / Executive View
Treat operations as an engineered organizational system. The objective is a repeatable mechanism that converts strategy into measurable outcomes through clear ownership, information flow, decision cadence, resource allocation, controls, and continuous feedback.
