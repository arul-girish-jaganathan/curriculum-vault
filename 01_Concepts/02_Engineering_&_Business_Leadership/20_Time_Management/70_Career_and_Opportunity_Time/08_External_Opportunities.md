# External Opportunities

## Purpose
Build a durable understanding of **External Opportunities** as part of a personal and organizational time-management operating system.

## Core Model
- **Time:** calendar capacity that cannot be recovered once spent.
- **Attention:** cognitive focus available for the work.
- **Energy:** physical and mental capacity that varies across the day and week.
- **Priority:** relative importance based on outcomes, values, risk, and opportunity cost.
- **Leverage:** the amount of future value created by an hour of work.

## Key Questions
1. What outcome does this time enable?
2. Is this work the highest-value use of the available capacity?
3. Does it require the person's direct involvement?
4. Can it be eliminated, simplified, automated, delegated, or moved?
5. What happens when the plan meets interruptions or unexpected demand?

## Design Principles
- Protect time for high-value work before low-value work fills the calendar.
- Match cognitive demand to energy and attention windows.
- Limit work in progress and context switching.
- Use explicit buffers for uncertainty and interruptions.
- Review actual time usage rather than relying only on intention.
- Treat the calendar as an execution system, not merely a record of appointments.

## Execution
A practical workflow is:
1. capture commitments;
2. clarify the desired outcome;
3. prioritize against current goals;
4. estimate effort and calendar duration;
5. place important work into appropriate time blocks;
6. leave capacity for uncertainty;
7. execute with focus;
8. review actual time and outcomes;
9. adjust the system.

## Failure Modes
Common problems include:
- overcommitting;
- false urgency;
- excessive meetings;
- notification-driven work;
- context switching;
- unclear next actions;
- underestimated effort;
- no recovery/buffer capacity;
- procrastination;
- perfectionism;
- failure to delegate;
- priorities changing without calendar changes.

## Measurement
Useful measures may include:
- focus hours;
- strategic hours;
- meeting hours;
- communication hours;
- reactive hours;
- deadline reliability;
- task aging;
- work-in-progress;
- time spent by goal/category;
- recovery and energy patterns.

Metrics should improve decisions, not create another administrative burden.

## Personal and Professional Boundaries
Time management must account for personal commitments, family responsibilities, health, recovery, travel, and professional demands. A sustainable system explicitly recognizes finite total capacity.

## Leadership / Executive Context
As responsibility increases, the unit of work changes. Individual contributors optimize tasks; leaders optimize decisions, people, systems, and organizational leverage. The objective is not to personally complete more work, but to spend scarce executive attention where it produces disproportionate value.

## Practical Review
Ask:
- What should I stop doing?
- What should I delegate?
- What deserves protected focus time?
- What recurring work can be standardized or automated?
- Where is my calendar inconsistent with my stated priorities?
- What commitment should be renegotiated before it becomes a failure?

## Common Mistakes
- Treating every request as equally important.
- Filling every calendar gap.
- Using meetings to solve problems that need ownership or documentation instead.
- Optimizing productivity while ignoring recovery.
- Measuring busyness rather than outcomes.
- Designing a system so complicated that maintaining it consumes the time it was meant to save.

## Staff-to-CEO Perspective
Time is an allocation problem. The more senior the role, the more important it becomes to convert personal time into organizational leverage through strategy, decisions, talent, delegation, systems, relationships, and capital allocation.
