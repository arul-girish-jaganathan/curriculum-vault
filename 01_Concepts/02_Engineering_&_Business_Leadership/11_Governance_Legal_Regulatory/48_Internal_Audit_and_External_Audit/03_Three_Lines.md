# Three Lines

## Purpose
Build a practical, executive-grade understanding of **Three Lines** as part of governance, legal, regulatory, risk, and corporate decision-making.

## Core Model
- **Obligation:** identify the law, regulation, contract, policy, standard, fiduciary duty, or governance expectation involved.
- **Scope:** identify the legal entity, geography, product, transaction, lifecycle stage, and stakeholders affected.
- **Accountability:** identify who owns the decision, who approves it, who executes it, and who provides independent assurance.
- **Evidence:** identify the records required to demonstrate compliance, reasonable process, or a defensible decision.
- **Risk:** distinguish legal exposure, regulatory exposure, financial impact, operational impact, safety/security impact, and reputational impact.

## Key Questions
1. What is actually mandatory, what is contractual, and what is guidance?
2. Which legal entity and jurisdiction are in scope?
3. What facts determine applicability or materiality?
4. Who has decision authority and escalation responsibility?
5. What evidence would an auditor, regulator, board, customer, court, or investigator need?

## Governance and Controls
Consider:
- policy and standard hierarchy;
- segregation of duties;
- delegated authority;
- decision records;
- control ownership;
- independent review;
- exception management;
- monitoring and assurance;
- escalation;
- corrective action.

## Evidence and Records
Strong governance depends on reliable records such as approvals, contracts, assessments, risk analyses, meeting records, training, test results, configuration history, audit trails, incident records, regulatory submissions, and management decisions.

## Change Management
Assess changes for:
- legal and regulatory applicability;
- product or market impact;
- entity/jurisdiction impact;
- contractual impact;
- safety/security/privacy impact;
- disclosure/materiality implications;
- required approvals and evidence.

## Risk and Materiality
Do not confuse legal compliance with acceptable business risk. A decision may be technically feasible but legally restricted, contractually prohibited, regulatorily reportable, or materially significant to customers/investors.

## Engineering / Product Considerations
For technology companies, connect governance to:
- product launch;
- architecture and design reviews;
- cybersecurity;
- software licensing;
- privacy;
- AI use;
- safety and reliability;
- supplier management;
- secure updates;
- incident response;
- field actions and recalls.

## Common Mistakes
- Treating a standard as automatically being law.
- Applying one jurisdiction's rule globally without checking local scope.
- Relying on undocumented verbal approvals.
- Ignoring entity-specific or product-specific obligations.
- Treating compliance as a document exercise rather than an operating control.
- Escalating only after a deadline, incident, or regulator contact.
- Assuming legal/compliance review replaces engineering ownership.

## Executive View
A strong governance system makes good decisions easier: clear authority, clear risk appetite, timely legal advice, independent assurance, auditable evidence, disciplined escalation, and explicit accountability.

## Staff-to-CEO View
The objective is not to become a lawyer. It is to know **which questions must be asked, which risks cannot be ignored, who must decide, what evidence must exist, and when a business decision becomes a board, regulatory, legal, or fiduciary matter.**
