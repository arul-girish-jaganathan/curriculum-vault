# Medical

## Purpose
Build a decision-oriented understanding of **Medical** within competitive intelligence, strategy, market analysis, and executive decision support.

## Core Model
- **Question:** what decision or uncertainty is being addressed?
- **Evidence:** what observable facts, source material, or signals support the analysis?
- **Inference:** what interpretation is being made from those facts?
- **Confidence:** how strong is the evidence and what could invalidate the conclusion?
- **Decision impact:** what action, scenario, or strategic choice could change because of the finding?

## Key Questions
1. What exactly is known versus inferred?
2. Which sources support the conclusion, and how independent are they?
3. What alternative explanations fit the same evidence?
4. How recent and decision-relevant is the information?
5. What would materially change the conclusion?

## Evidence Discipline
Separate:
- direct facts from interpretation;
- primary evidence from secondary reporting;
- leading indicators from lagging indicators;
- observed behavior from stated strategy;
- correlation from causation.

Use triangulation when a decision is material. Record source provenance and confidence rather than presenting uncertain inference as fact.

## Competitive Interpretation
Evaluate the implications for customers, products, technology, pricing, channels, supply chain, talent, capital allocation, partnerships, and market position as applicable.

## Analytical Workflow
1. Frame the intelligence question.
2. Define the relevant entities, market, time horizon, and decision.
3. Gather high-quality sources.
4. Normalize and validate the evidence.
5. Identify changes, patterns, gaps, and contradictions.
6. Develop competing explanations or scenarios.
7. Assess confidence and strategic implications.
8. Communicate the conclusion with clear evidence.
9. Track what happens next and update the assessment.

## Failure Modes
Common intelligence failures include:
- confirmation bias;
- competitor marketing being treated as fact;
- stale data;
- single-source conclusions;
- weak market definitions;
- double counting;
- false precision in market sizing;
- confusing activity with strategic importance;
- ignoring second-order effects;
- overreacting to noisy signals.

## Quantitative Considerations
Where numeric analysis is relevant, define units, period, denominator, geography, segment, and source. State assumptions behind market size, growth, share, pricing, margins, capacity, or forecast calculations.

## Executive Communication
A useful output answers:
- **What changed?**
- **Why does it matter?**
- **What evidence supports it?**
- **How confident are we?**
- **What should management watch or do next?**

Keep factual evidence distinguishable from strategic recommendation.

## Embedded / Technology Context
For technology-intensive companies, consider product roadmaps, semiconductor availability, toolchains, architectures, patents, developer ecosystems, engineering hiring, manufacturing capacity, standards, and supply-chain dependencies.

## Ethics and Governance
Use lawful and appropriate sources. Do not rely on confidential information, insider information, impersonation, unauthorized access, or deceptive collection methods. Respect source terms, privacy, and contractual restrictions.

## Common Mistakes
- Collecting information without a decision question.
- Measuring a competitor only through its marketing claims.
- Treating a job posting as proof of a final product.
- Treating patent filings as proof of commercial deployment.
- Using an isolated financial metric without context.
- Making market-size claims without transparent assumptions.
- Delivering facts without an implication or decision relevance.

## Staff / Executive View
Competitive intelligence is useful when it changes decisions. Build repeatable mechanisms that convert weak signals and verified facts into scenario awareness, strategic choices, early warnings, and resource-allocation decisions.
