# Architecture Influence

## Purpose
Develop a practical, executive-grade understanding of **Architecture Influence** as a tool for negotiation, influence, stakeholder alignment, conflict resolution, and leadership.

## Core Model
- **Objective:** define the outcome that matters and how success will be measured.
- **Interests:** understand your needs, the other side's needs, constraints, incentives, and risks.
- **Leverage:** identify alternatives, dependencies, information, authority, relationships, timing, and credibility.
- **Communication:** make the message clear, adaptive, and evidence-based.
- **Relationship:** protect trust and long-term value where the relationship matters.
- **Decision:** define commitments, ownership, timing, and walk-away conditions.

## Preparation
Before an important conversation, clarify:
- desired outcome and acceptable range;
- BATNA and reservation point;
- counterpart interests and likely constraints;
- issues that can be traded rather than argued;
- evidence and objective criteria;
- possible concessions and what you require in return;
- timing, decision authority, and escalation path.

## Conversation Questions
1. What does each side actually need?
2. What is being assumed but not verified?
3. Who decides, who influences, and who can block?
4. What happens if no agreement is reached?
5. Which issues can be bundled to create value?
6. What commitment should exist at the end of the conversation?

## Influence
Influence without authority comes from combinations of expertise, credibility, trust, relationships, evidence, narrative, reciprocity, consistency, coalition building, and understanding the other side's incentives.

## Communication
Prefer clear framing, active listening, calibrated questions, explicit assumptions, concise options and tradeoffs, and written confirmation of decisions and commitments. Listen for interests, emotions, contradictions, constraints, and decision criteria.

## Conflict
Separate people from the problem. Diagnose whether conflict is driven by interests, information, resources, process, values, incentives, status, power, or unclear decision rights. Resolve the root cause where practical rather than merely forcing agreement.

## Tradeoffs and Concessions
Concessions should be deliberate. Trade value across multiple issues and prefer conditional reciprocity over one-sided giving. Do not negotiate only on headline price when scope, quality, timing, risk, service, IP, payment terms, or strategic value can be exchanged.

## Executive Context
For executive negotiations, connect the discussion to business value, customer impact, financial outcomes, risk, strategic timing, organizational capacity, long-term relationships, governance, and accountability.

## Decision Quality
Distinguish facts, estimates, assumptions, and preferences. Use expected value, scenario analysis, reversibility, opportunity cost, and information value where useful. Do not manufacture certainty to make a decision look stronger.

## Ethics
Influence should not require deception, coercion, humiliation, or exploitation. Protect confidentiality, avoid misrepresentation, and recognize when a deal should be rejected because the long-term risk or ethical cost is unacceptable.

## Common Mistakes
- Negotiating without knowing your BATNA.
- Fighting positions instead of discovering interests.
- Making concessions without reciprocity.
- Using authority when influence is needed.
- Talking too much and asking too few questions.
- Treating every negotiation as zero-sum.
- Ignoring implementation and follow-through.
- Winning the conversation while damaging the relationship.

## Practice
For important conversations, record context, interests, leverage, opening, key questions, tradeoffs, concessions, decision points, outcome, and what you would do differently next time.

## Staff / CEO-Level View
Treat negotiation and influence as organizational systems. The goal is not to "win" individual conversations; it is to improve decision quality, create durable alignment, allocate resources intelligently, protect relationships, and move the organization toward strategic outcomes.
