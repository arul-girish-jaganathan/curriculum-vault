# Budget Scope

## Purpose
Build a practical, executive-level understanding of **Budget Scope** as a component of organizational design and operating-model decisions.

## Core Model
- **Purpose:** connect organizational choices to strategy, customers, value creation, and execution.
- **Structure:** define grouping, reporting, roles, authority, and boundaries.
- **Decision rights:** identify who decides, who recommends, who executes, and who is accountable.
- **Information flow:** determine how signals, decisions, context, and feedback move through the organization.
- **Incentives:** identify behaviors the structure and measurement system will encourage.
- **Tradeoffs:** make the cost of complexity, coordination, speed, control, specialization, and autonomy explicit.

## Key Questions
1. What business problem is the organizational choice solving?
2. Which capabilities and decisions need to be close to customers, products, technology, or operations?
3. Where are the current bottlenecks, handoffs, conflicts, and duplicated responsibilities?
4. What should be centralized, decentralized, shared, or embedded?
5. How will the design be measured and adjusted after implementation?

## Structure and Accountability
Define:
- team and function mission;
- role responsibilities;
- solid and dotted reporting where relevant;
- decision ownership;
- escalation paths;
- interfaces between functions;
- budget and resource ownership;
- success metrics.

Avoid structures where accountability is shared so widely that nobody can make or own a decision.

## Operating Model
Connect organizational structure with:
- planning and prioritization;
- product/service delivery;
- architecture and technical governance;
- quality and risk management;
- hiring and talent systems;
- financial/resource allocation;
- customer feedback;
- operational reviews.

A good operating model reduces unnecessary coordination while keeping the right expertise and control where it is needed.

## Failure Modes
Common organizational failures include:
- unclear ownership;
- too many approval layers;
- excessive matrix complexity;
- duplicated capabilities;
- centralized bottlenecks;
- local optimization;
- incentive conflicts;
- weak cross-functional interfaces;
- overloaded managers;
- key-person dependency;
- reorganization without process or decision changes;
- metrics that reward the wrong behavior.

## Decision Design
Separate:
- reversible vs irreversible decisions;
- strategic vs tactical decisions;
- local vs enterprise decisions;
- expertise-based vs authority-based decisions.

Push decisions toward the lowest competent level that has sufficient context, while retaining enterprise-level governance where consistency, risk, or scale requires it.

## Metrics
Useful signals may include:
- decision latency;
- execution speed;
- cross-team dependency count;
- manager span of control;
- attrition in critical roles;
- hiring time and quality;
- customer outcomes;
- engineering throughput/quality;
- operating cost;
- employee health/engagement signals.

Avoid turning metrics into targets without understanding how they may change behavior.

## Change and Implementation
Organizational design is incomplete without transition planning:
1. establish the case for change;
2. define design principles;
3. map the current state;
4. design the target state;
5. assess people and capability gaps;
6. define transition ownership;
7. communicate decisions and rationale;
8. stabilize and measure;
9. adjust based on evidence.

## Engineering / Technology Context
For technology organizations, explicitly consider product vs platform boundaries, architecture governance, hardware/software interfaces, R&D vs product engineering, manufacturing connections, safety/security/compliance independence, technical career ladders, and Staff/Principal leadership paths.

## Common Mistakes
- Copying another company's org chart without its context.
- Optimizing headcount cost while increasing coordination cost.
- Creating committees instead of clarifying decision rights.
- Using dotted lines to hide accountability problems.
- Promoting managers as the only path for senior technical growth.
- Reorganizing repeatedly instead of fixing operating mechanisms.
- Treating culture as a poster instead of an incentive-and-behavior system.

## Staff and CEO View
Organizational design is a systems problem. The objective is not the prettiest hierarchy; it is a structure that creates clear ownership, fast high-quality decisions, strong capabilities, healthy incentives, manageable coordination, customer value, and durable execution.
