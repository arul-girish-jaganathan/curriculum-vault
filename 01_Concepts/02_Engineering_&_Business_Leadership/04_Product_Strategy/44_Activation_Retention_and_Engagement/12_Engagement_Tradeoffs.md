# Engagement Tradeoffs

## Purpose
Build a durable executive-level understanding of **Engagement Tradeoffs** and how it affects customer value, product strategy, business economics, execution, and long-term company advantage.

## Core Model
- **Customer:** who experiences the problem or receives the value.
- **Problem:** what need, friction, risk, or opportunity exists.
- **Product response:** what the company chooses to build, offer, price, distribute, or deliberately not do.
- **Business effect:** how the decision changes revenue, margin, growth, retention, strategic position, or risk.
- **Evidence:** what data or customer behavior supports the decision.

## Strategic Questions
1. Which customer and segment matter most?
2. What evidence says the problem is important and persistent?
3. Why should the company win rather than an incumbent, substitute, or internal alternative?
4. What tradeoffs and opportunity costs are created?
5. Which metric or business outcome will show whether the strategy is working?

## Customer and Market Evidence
Distinguish customer statements from observed behavior. Use qualitative research, quantitative data, usage patterns, win/loss evidence, competitive analysis, and market signals as appropriate. Make assumptions explicit and identify what evidence would change the decision.

## Product and Business Tradeoffs
Evaluate:
- customer value;
- differentiation;
- time to market;
- revenue and margin;
- acquisition and retention;
- support/operational cost;
- technology/platform leverage;
- safety, security, privacy, and compliance;
- organizational capacity;
- lifecycle and migration cost.

## Metrics
Separate:
- outcome metrics from activity metrics;
- leading indicators from lagging indicators;
- customer metrics from internal operational metrics;
- strategic metrics from diagnostic metrics.

A metric is useful when it drives a better decision rather than merely producing a dashboard.

## Decision Quality
A strong product decision states:
- context;
- objective;
- alternatives;
- criteria;
- assumptions;
- evidence;
- recommendation;
- risks;
- ownership;
- review date.

Prefer explicit tradeoffs over vague consensus.

## Failure Modes
Watch for:
- feature-factory behavior;
- roadmap commitments without validated outcomes;
- vanity metrics;
- solving for loud customers rather than valuable segments;
- confusing user preference with willingness to pay;
- underestimating switching costs;
- over-discounting;
- scaling before product-market fit;
- ignoring operational and lifecycle costs;
- optimizing a local metric while damaging the business system.

## Execution and Lifecycle
Connect strategy to discovery, delivery, launch, adoption, retention, expansion, support, reliability, pricing, migration, and eventual product retirement. A strategy that cannot survive operational reality is incomplete.

## Executive View
Consider second-order effects, portfolio interactions, competitor responses, capital requirements, organizational consequences, and reversibility. Treat product strategy as a sequence of choices about where to invest, where to learn, and where to stop.

## Embedded / Hardware Product Considerations
For physical or embedded products, include BOM cost, certification, manufacturing capacity, firmware updateability, field reliability, serviceability, long development cycles, platform reuse, hardware/software variants, supply-chain constraints, and installed-base migration.

## Common Mistakes
- Starting with the feature instead of the customer problem.
- Treating TAM as proof of demand.
- Confusing product-market fit with revenue growth alone.
- Using prioritization frameworks as substitutes for judgment.
- Treating a roadmap as strategy.
- Ignoring pricing and unit economics until late.
- Measuring activity instead of customer/business outcomes.

## Staff / Executive Perspective
Translate product decisions into strategic consequences: customer value, economic value, competitive position, risk, capital allocation, organizational load, and lifecycle effects. The goal is not to maximize features; it is to maximize durable value creation while preserving strategic options.
