# Debt Schedule

## Purpose
Build a practical, executive-grade understanding of **Debt Schedule** within corporate development and M&A.

## Core Model
- **Strategic objective:** what business problem or strategic opportunity is being addressed?
- **Value:** where does economic or strategic value come from?
- **Risk:** what can invalidate the thesis or destroy value?
- **Evidence:** what information supports the conclusion?
- **Decision:** what should management, the investment committee, or the board decide?

## Key Questions
1. Why this target, transaction, partnership, or structure?
2. What is the standalone value versus strategic value?
3. Which assumptions are most important to the deal thesis?
4. What risks are material enough to change price, structure, protections, or the decision to walk away?
5. How will value be realized after signing and closing?

## Analysis
Separate:
- facts from management claims;
- assumptions from evidence;
- standalone economics from synergy economics;
- one-time effects from recurring economics;
- risks from mitigations;
- signing value from post-close realized value.

## Due Diligence
Use focused workstreams appropriate to the transaction: strategy, product, technology, engineering, security, customers, commercial, finance, accounting, legal, regulatory, tax, people, operations, supply chain, manufacturing, quality, safety, and compliance.

The objective is not to collect documents. It is to answer decision-critical questions and surface issues early enough to affect the transaction.

## Valuation and Deal Economics
Evaluate:
- enterprise value and equity value;
- revenue/earnings multiples where appropriate;
- discounted cash flow and scenario analysis;
- net debt and working-capital adjustments;
- capital intensity and free cash flow;
- synergy value and execution probability;
- financing capacity and dilution;
- risk-adjusted valuation range.

## Negotiation and Structure
Deal terms allocate economics, control, risk, and incentives. Consider consideration type, earnouts, rollover equity, indemnities, escrow, governance rights, financing, conditions precedent, regulatory risk, and seller/buyer incentives.

## Integration
A successful transaction requires explicit ownership for:
- Day One readiness;
- leadership and organization;
- product and technology;
- engineering;
- IT/data/security;
- finance/HR/legal;
- sales and customers;
- supply chain/operations;
- culture and talent;
- synergy realization.

## Failure Modes
Common failures include:
- strategic rationale that is too vague;
- overpaying for expected synergies;
- weak diligence evidence;
- hidden liabilities;
- optimistic forecasts;
- customer or talent attrition;
- underestimated integration complexity;
- incompatible cultures or operating models;
- technology debt discovered after closing;
- regulatory or contractual constraints;
- synergy leakage;
- unclear post-close accountability.

## Governance and Evidence
Important decisions should have a clear record of:
- strategic rationale;
- valuation assumptions;
- alternatives considered;
- diligence findings;
- material risks;
- mitigations;
- negotiated tradeoffs;
- approval path;
- integration commitments.

## Executive Communication
Strong deal communication is decision-oriented. Explain what matters, why it matters, what is uncertain, what could go wrong, the economic range, and what management proposes to do.

## Common Mistakes
- Treating headline revenue growth as proof of value.
- Treating management presentations as equivalent to diligence evidence.
- Valuing synergies without execution cost or probability.
- Ignoring customer concentration and key-person dependencies.
- Underestimating integration and separation work.
- Focusing on purchase price while ignoring total lifecycle cost.
- Confusing strategic excitement with risk-adjusted return.

## Technology and Engineering Perspective
For technology acquisitions, assess architecture, codebase quality, IP ownership, security, technical debt, scalability, build/toolchain, testing, cloud/infrastructure, engineering organization, roadmap credibility, and the cost of integration or continued independence.

## Staff-Level View
Think like an owner of the whole transaction: connect strategy, market structure, diligence evidence, valuation, deal terms, financing, integration, synergies, risk, governance, and long-term portfolio value. The goal is not simply to close a deal; it is to create durable enterprise value.
