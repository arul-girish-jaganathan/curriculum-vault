# Platform Scale

## Purpose
Build a durable executive-level understanding of **Platform Scale** as an AI technology and business-economics concept.

## Core Model
- **Technology:** what capability, infrastructure, workflow, or model behavior creates value.
- **Economics:** what costs scale with usage, capacity, customers, or complexity.
- **Value capture:** who pays, what they pay for, and where margin accumulates.
- **Strategic position:** what creates differentiation, switching costs, scale, or ecosystem power.
- **Risk:** what could destroy the expected value or change the economics.

## Key Questions
1. What customer or business outcome does this create?
2. Which costs are fixed, variable, capacity-driven, or step-function costs?
3. How does economics change with scale and utilization?
4. What creates or erodes the moat?
5. Which assumptions must be true for the strategy to work?

## Economic Analysis
Evaluate:
- revenue model;
- variable cost and contribution margin;
- fixed infrastructure cost;
- utilization and capacity;
- customer acquisition and retention;
- payback period;
- capital intensity;
- opportunity cost;
- sensitivity to technology and market changes.

## Technology-Economics Link
Trace the causal chain from technical design to business outcome. Examples include model size -> inference cost, context length -> token cost, accelerator utilization -> unit economics, latency -> conversion, automation rate -> labor savings, data quality -> model value, and reliability -> customer retention.

## Scale Effects
Determine whether scale creates:
- economies of scale;
- better utilization;
- learning effects;
- network effects;
- purchasing power;
- data advantages;
- lower unit costs;
or instead creates congestion, coordination cost, capital requirements, regulatory exposure, or diminishing returns.

## Competitive Analysis
Compare:
- incumbent advantage;
- new entrant advantage;
- open-source alternatives;
- vertically integrated competitors;
- platform ecosystems;
- customer build-vs-buy options;
- substitutes such as traditional software or human labor.

## Decision Framework
1. Define the strategic objective.
2. Identify the customer and economic driver.
3. Quantify the major cost and value variables.
4. Identify technology dependencies.
5. Model base, upside, and downside cases.
6. Assess reversibility and optionality.
7. Decide what to build, buy, partner, or avoid.
8. Define leading indicators that would trigger a strategy change.

## Risk
Consider model quality, supply constraints, compute availability, energy, regulation, security, privacy, vendor concentration, talent scarcity, demand uncertainty, margin compression, and technology substitution.

## Executive Metrics
Typical metrics include revenue, gross margin, contribution margin, usage, retention, expansion, CAC, LTV, payback, cost per inference, utilization, capacity, latency, quality, adoption, and ROI. Select only metrics that inform a decision.

## Common Mistakes
- Confusing technical capability with customer value.
- Assuming model quality automatically creates pricing power.
- Ignoring inference and infrastructure costs.
- Using averages that hide customer or workload distribution.
- Building capacity before demand is proven.
- Treating market growth as guaranteed company growth.
- Mistaking temporary scarcity for a durable moat.
- Ignoring substitutes and build-vs-buy alternatives.

## Long-Term View
AI economics can change rapidly as models improve, hardware becomes more efficient, open models spread, infrastructure scales, and workflows change. Separate durable strategic principles from assumptions that should be revisited frequently.

## CEO-Level View
Think across the entire stack: technology, customer value, unit economics, capital intensity, competition, organization, regulation, supply chain, and geopolitical risk. The objective is not to own the most advanced technology; it is to build a durable enterprise where technology creates superior economic value.
